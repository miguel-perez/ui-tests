# What goes through people's minds when they try to find out whether others can actually use what they built, and what gets in the way?

Study D0, 15 October 2026. 124 public first-person accounts (Stack Exchange and Hacker News posts, 2009 to 2026), 523 summaries of moment-bound thinking, grouped by shared purpose into 27 groups, 7 broader concerns and 3 top groups. Counts below are of distinct accounts, not of summaries. Group membership and the per-group counts trace to `groups.csv`, which is retained. Summary-level references (`s123`) pointed at `summaries.csv` and `rows.csv`, which were purged on 15 October 2026 with the raw accounts; they identify which summary a claim came from but can no longer be checked against the text without regenerating the study (see `D0-purposes.md`).

## The short answer

People want to see other people use the thing, because they know they can no longer see it themselves. Most of them never get to. They post instead.

Eighty-five of 124 accounts contain thinking about finding out from real people whether what they built works for them. Of those, 32 describe the moment of watching someone use it and being shown something they could not have seen alone. Twenty-six describe turning to a forum because they have no reference to lean on, and for many of them the post is the test. What stands between the want and the watching is, in order of how many people hit it: not knowing how to run a fair test, not being able to afford or reach a tool, not having people who match the audience, and not having anyone around them who thinks it matters.

## 1. The want: outside eyes, because mine are blind to it

Twenty-one people say some version of "I can't see it fresh any more." A first-time web developer "find[s] it hard to look at the page with fresh eyes" (hn_2504617, s45). A freelancer has a senior designer review the site "on purpose" because he has never been on a larger team (se_ux_55233, s474). Two founders consider applying to YC early because "we ourselves are not the best judge of our work" (hn_2134380, s54). A hardware builder realises "the way I build hardware projects ... is only a tiny fragment of how other makers and engineers build" and enlists 173 ideal buyers to vote (hn_22628988, s58, s59).

Nine people explicitly discount signals that are not observed behaviour. Friends' excitement is "to be expected" (hn_1845594, s12); surveys are rejected because "random people expressing interest is not the same as real customers buying" (s13); the sole designer on a team knows users "will tell me what they expect I want to hear" (se_ux_81741, s493); an enterprise UXer bases decisions on "actual behavior" over attitudes (se_ux_87364, s462). The counterexamples matter: four people took stated liking as their evidence (hn_2134380 s50, hn_8237001 s244, se_softwareengineering_378166 s274), and the one whose app then failed "didn't even know why" (s245). Seven people describe the later realisation that they built on assumptions with no feedback loop (hn_38533620 s23 to s25, hn_36227470 s84 to s86, hn_11241608 s100).

## 2. The moment: watching shows what nothing else does

Thirty-two accounts contain the moment itself, and it is the strongest material in the study. A developer usability-testing API tutorials: "really helpful to see people charge off down the wrong alley if the wording is just slightly off," rewritten four times as a result (hn_4302433, s110, s111). Weebly's two-field sign-up form: users "stare at the page for minutes" because it does not look like a sign-up form (hn_942344, s264). A slide tool learns that people prefer drafts to realtime, "this surprised us" (hn_6023966, s222, s223). A pub-quiz builder changes things from flexible to simpler after every hallway test because "people can't become power users if they aren't users" (hn_24463463, s22). A writing-helper author knows latency matters but feels it "differently when I watch my own users hesitate" (hn_46409307, s145). Someone watching end users on a rewrite calls the information "gold" (hn_3742480, s96). The accessible first-aid simulator's first test with blind testers produced a "cacophony" despite following every recommendation (se_ux_118374, s363 to s365).

Fifteen people then change or simplify the thing because of what people could not get through (G6). Ten describe weighing what a finding means before acting: whether a problem is real or novelty (se_ux_142593, s435), whether a small sample counts (se_ux_141842, s431), whether a rating has any quantifiable meaning (se_ux_91932, s473).

## 3. What gets in the way

**Not knowing how to run a fair test (38 people across G9a, G9b, G3b, G10).** A developer who "decided to do things right" asks "How does one do so? ... What questions should I ask? How should I observe them? Should I have a plan?" (hn_2726997, s3, s5). Seven people work through task wording, scripts and setup; the recurring fear is that a too-specific goal leads the user and a too-open one yields incomparable results (se_ux_95050, s508). A first-timer is "uncomfortable about the script" (se_ux_122318, s380). A moderator points out the zoom tool mid-test and then does not know whether she should have (se_ux_20222, s454, s455). Twenty-six people turn to a forum for the how-to or for validation, and for several the forum reply is the only outside judgement they get (se_ux_82075 s499 to s501, se_ux_100152 s320 to s322, se_ux_40286 s424).

**Tools that block or price out individuals and small teams (6 people, vivid).** A student finds Maze "perfect" until the 7-board limit, then the price doubles at checkout for a mandatory second seat, then every other tool wants a company email or $500 a month: "I am a student. I am broke." (se_ux_141621, s413 to s417). A company-wide tester finds every candidate "unstable, feature-poor, or horrible in usability" and every framework a "nogo" for non-developers (hn_5036553, s183, s185). Six people build their own tool because nothing does what they need, including three who build AI-persona testers precisely because they have too few real users (hn_46913402 s122 to s125, hn_48647957 s149 to s151, hn_46510066 s118, s119).

**No participants who match, or no way to get the build to them (13 people).** A paid panel's testers "rushed through everything" and all but one failed a simple requirement (hn_1783588, s8, s9). A free Peek test was "not really helpful" because the tester was not the target audience (hn_7535505, s229). Startups cannot get the right alpha users without launching (hn_2654053, s17, s18), friends cannot reach the dev server (se_stackoverflow_3328926, s280), future customers should not be made to endure the UDID hassle (hn_4724698, s156), and the person with the biggest user base has no access to it (se_ux_17974, s447).

**Is it worth it (7 people), and the tension with building and promoting (8).** A career-changer weighs 15 to 20 participants against a portfolio goal (se_ux_131516, s405 to s410). A consultant finds testing "the same problems I've detected through expert review" and feels resources wasted (se_ux_132241, s412). A founder feels "a little lost" between acquiring users and developing the product, even though testing was "a wonderful revelation" (hn_8231452, s239, s240). Another launched a week ago and fears the site is too incomplete to promote, yet gets no feedback without promotion (hn_831533, s248).

**Nobody around them thinks it matters (9 people).** A designer whose last company had a researcher now has a PO deciding "only on his intuition" and proposes to carry all research herself on top of design (se_ux_145133, s1, s2). A developer with "no in-house usability analysts" finds "nobody seems very interested" (se_stackoverflow_424589, s297, s298). A product team exhausted by developers who "tear into a new feature in the meeting" has "no qualitative or quantitative data to show them" (se_ux_149719, s441, s444). A student's objection is dismissed because the teacher is "the pro" (se_ux_129811, s312).

## 4. The want to know continuously, and the agent substitute

Nine people want to know in the build loop or in production, not in a one-off session. "QA and unit tests tell me whether the code works but not whether the experience makes sense" (hn_46913402, s123). A startup changes its "whole approach towards testing" after a bug that only a real user with more than five files would hit (hn_42157991, s81, s82). A beta owner "would pay good money" for a recording of each user's ten-second sign-up (hn_5688018, s197). An MCP builder realises "we have no idea how our users are using our MCP or whether they are satisfied" (hn_49157807, s207), and another has "no reliable way to test our high-stakes enterprise integrations the way we test normal software, or to know whether they work once they reach real users" (hn_49745351, s179). One person reads agent logs as "speed-running hundreds of usability tests with people" (hn_48472297, s137); another watches an agent run "a full usability test" and self-correct (hn_48784968, s152, s153). These accounts never claim the agent stands in for a person; they use agents because people are unavailable.

## 5. What people did when they could not test

Twelve people reason out whether users will understand a control from their own judgement, for lack of a test: whether icons read as clickable (se_ux_100152), whether a profile picture reads as a button (se_ux_82075), whether a jog dial will "go down" with Android users (se_ux_106227). Nine rig up something cheap: a head-mounted GoPro as "a poor man's version of eye tracking" that then documents user mistakes "in HD as proof to the programmers" (se_ux_80282, s482, s486); cheap BLU phones so that device problems are not overlooked in an office of flagships (hn_20940096, s48); a $15 ad to see if the site "stands up to a little user testing" (hn_7963052, s231); a Google Analytics goal as a usability test of a sign-up page (se_stackoverflow_37090281, s293).

## 6. Differences and counterexamples

- One person deliberately runs no scenarios and measures nothing, because "the most useful sessions go way off the rails" (hn_17339903; no moment-bound rows, views only).
- One holds that in-person testing is "extremely qualitative and unscientific" and that webcams and eye-tracking lend unwarranted "scientific-ness" (hn_6072635, views only).
- One team hired a UX architect for live testing instead of buying a walkthrough tool and judged it "well worth the money" (hn_5588804, s192 to s194).
- Two people prefer expert review or their own hunch over testing for cost reasons (se_ux_132241, se_ux_131516).
- One person tested a word processor with a single eight-year-old and "pronounced our endeavor a success" (hn_4441609, s117), then was buried in complaints after the next release.

## Limits

These are people who posted, so the sample over-represents those who were stuck or proud enough to write, and under-represents people who tested quietly and moved on. Stack Exchange accounts skew to UX practitioners and students; Hacker News accounts skew to founders and solo developers. The accounts report thinking at one moment, almost never an outcome. Counts describe this set of 124 accounts and nothing beyond it. Three summaries were set aside as decisions about how to write the post rather than about finding out (s21, s56, s352).
